# E4 and E5 — do the dials carry intent, and can a model write them?

*2026-10-29. Companion to `E0-prior-art.md`. The design under test is
`docs/design/vox-score.md`; the analyzer is `tools/vox-ref/`; the harness is
`tools/vox-e4/`.*

## 0. What was run, in one table

| step | what | result |
|---|---|---|
| E1 (bonus) | analyzer against synthetic signals with known truth | **5/5 checks pass** — F0 within 0.05 cents, formants within 2.1%, onset within 9.5 ms, bit-identical across runs |
| dataset | 340 skald/Riva clips: 20 lines × 3 speakers × 6 delivery styles | 414 s of audio, 0 render failures |
| ears | LFM3-Audio transcribes every clip | **96.5% exact** (328/340), median 182 ms, p95 421 ms |
| analysis | reference analyzer on every clip | 73 ms per clip, CPU only |
| **E4** | 6 gate arms + 1 LFM3 arm, two gates each | **partial pass.** The dials read delivery at **82.2%** (urgent vs not) where the words are stuck at **33.3%** and LFM3's own audio understanding at **60.6%** — and they do it in 105 ms against 1518 ms. The L2 line as the design writes it is much weaker (53.9%); made profile-relative it is not. |
| **E5** | d1-2B writes a delivery, it is rendered and re-analysed | **fail against the 80% bar (52.2%)**, but it decomposes: the model picks the right delivery 65.2% of the time (16.7% chance), and a *correct* render is measured back correctly only 73.9% of the time. The measurement, not the model, is the binding constraint. |

Everything is reproducible from the repository with the scripts named in
§7. The evidence is in `evidence/vox-e4/`.

## 1. The dataset

`tools/vox-e4/render_dataset.py` renders the grid through the skald daemon
(`http://localhost:8000/v1/audio/speech`), which fronts Riva 2.26.

* **20 lines** — ten orders ("Hold the line.", "Move to the beacon.", …) and
  ten questions ("Do you see the beacon?", "Where is the enemy?", …).
* **3 speakers** — `Mia`, `Jason`, `Aria` (`Magpie-Multilingual.EN-US.*`).
* **6 styles** — `Default`, `Neutral`, `Calm`, `Happy`, `Angry`, `Sad`.
  `Sad` is missing for Jason, so the grid is 340 rather than 360 clips.
* 170 order clips and 170 question clips, exactly balanced.
* 414 seconds of audio in total.

Every line was checked against LFM3's ASR *before* the grid was rendered
(`tools/vox-e4/probe_utterances.py`): 20 of 22 candidates transcribed
exactly, and the two that did not ("Bring the rover around." → "Bring them
over around."; "Hold at the treeline." → "Hold at the tree line.") were
dropped. The lexical channel has to work, or the words-only arm would be
measuring ASR error rather than dials.

### 1.1 What skald can actually control

This matters more than it first appears, and it constrains E5.

| axis | controllable? | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **delivery style** (calm / angry / happy / sad / neutral) | **yes** | Riva exposes each style as its own voice id; the clips differ audibly and in the measured channels (§3) |
| **punctuation** | yes, weakly | the text is passed through, so "Hold the line." and "Hold the line!" differ |
| **SSML `<prosody>`** | **no** | Riva 2.26's REST schema has no `ssml` field, and posting SSML fails at inference: `Triton model failed during inference … magpie_tts_ensemble` |
| **pitch / rate / volume, numerically** | **no** | there is no parameter to set them with |

So the renderer's controllable axis is a **discrete set of styles**, not a
continuous performance. That is the single biggest constraint on the whole
design: today the L2 patch cannot be *rendered* faithfully, only *described*.

### 1.2 Riva's synthesis is not deterministic

The same text rendered twice gives two different waveforms, and even two
different durations (a 0.42 s and a 1.53 s rendering of "Hold the line." in
the same style were both observed). A corpus of clips is therefore a corpus
of *deliveries*, not of repeated stimuli. Every number below is therefore a
statement about a distribution of deliveries, not about identical audio, and
the "same words, different delivery" contrast is the honest one: the words
are held fixed, the realization is not.

Single-request render latency is **1.7–2.0 s**. (The 340-clip render used
8 concurrent workers and shows a much larger per-request figure of 11.8 s
median; that is queueing, not the service.)

## 2. The analyzer, and whether it measures what it claims

`tools/vox-ref/` implements L0 (100 Hz frames), L1 (gestures) and L2 (the
patch line). Full method in `tools/vox-ref/README.md`.

### 2.1 E1: accuracy against known truth

`python3 tools/vox-ref/tests/test_synth.py` → **5/5 pass**.

| check | tolerance (design §9) | measured |
|---|---|---|
| F0 at 80 / 110 / 150 / 200 / 300 Hz | ±10 cents | **≤ 0.05 cents** |
| Formants at 700 / 1300 / 2600 Hz | ±5% | **2.08% / 0.19% / 0.57%** |
| Onset of an isolated burst | ±10 ms | **9.5 ms** |
| Gesture count on four bursts | exact | **4** |
| Determinism (same bytes twice) | bit-identical | **identical** |

The onset check passes at 9.5 ms — inside the tolerance, but with almost no
margin, because the onset is declared on the frame *after* the spectral flux
rises. On a 100 Hz frame grid the honest resolution is ±10 ms and the
tolerance is exactly one frame.

### 2.2 Cost

73 ms per clip on one CPU core, no GPU and no model download: a 1.5 s
utterance is analysed in about 5% of real time.

## 3. What the measured channels show

Medians over the 340 clips, from `evidence/vox-e4/l2.csv`. `velocity` is the
peak level relative to the speaker's own profile; `brightness` is the
spectral centroid relative to the profile centroid.

| channel | Angry | Calm | Default | Happy | Neutral | Sad |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `vel` (dB vs profile) | **+4.71** | −2.63 | −0.65 | +4.26 | −4.64 | +2.39 |
| `tempo` (syllables/s) | 4.66 | **3.89** | 4.90 | 4.57 | 4.49 | 4.77 |
| `atk` (ms) | 87 | 95 | 77 | 85 | 82 | 87 |
| `duration` (s) | 1.02 | **1.56** | 0.86 | 1.03 | 0.85 | 1.04 |
| `gestures` | 5 | 6 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 |
| `contour` change (cents) | −654 | −350 | −868 | −684 | −542 | −432 |
| `brightness` (Hz vs profile) | **+595** | −159 | −54 | +343 | −371 | +378 |

Three things fall out of this, and they are the substance of E4:

1. **Arousal is loud.** Angry and Happy are +4.7 and +4.3 dB above the
   speaker's own peak; Neutral is −4.6 dB. The gap between the high-arousal
   and low-arousal styles is about 9 dB with a per-clip spread of ~1.8 dB.
2. **Calm is slow and long, not quiet.** Calm has the lowest tempo (3.89
   syllables/s) and by far the longest duration (1.56 s against Neutral's
   0.85 s). It is *softer* than Angry but only 2 dB below Neutral, so
   loudness alone does not separate them — **duration and tempo do**.
3. **Timbre carries a third, partly independent axis.** Angry is +595 Hz
   brighter than the speaker's norm and Neutral is −371 Hz. Brightness
   separates Angry from Neutral even where loudness is close.

### 3.1 How much class information is in the measurement?

A nearest-centroid classifier over six measured features
(`velocity_db`, `tempo_syl_per_s`, `attack_ms`, `duration_s`,
`contour_change_cents`, `brightness_offset_hz`), fitted on the 340 clips
and checked leave-one-speaker-out — the features are standardised on the
training speakers only, so the held-out speaker is never seen:

| task | in-sample | leave-one-speaker-out |
|---|---|---|
| 6 styles (every Riva style) | 58.2% | **49.7%** |
| 3 delivery classes, ordinary = Neutral (the E4 question) | 90.6% | **83.3%** |
| 3 delivery classes, ordinary = every other style | — | 68.1% |
| urgent vs not-urgent | — | 75.3% |

Two things to read here.

**The six-style number is a ceiling, and a low one.** At 60 clips per class,
with Angry/Happy/Sad all louder than Calm/Neutral, the styles overlap too
much to be told apart by level, tempo and timbre alone. Collapsing the
styles onto the three delivery classes the brief uses, with the broad
reading of "ordinary", the figure is 68.1%; with the narrow reading
(ordinary = Neutral only, which is what the E4 gate's vocabulary means) it
is 83.3%.

**The information is there.** A trivial classifier over the measured
channels gets 83.3% on the E4 delivery task, on speakers it has never seen.
This is the number to keep in mind for §4: if a reader of the L2 *line*
does far worse than a classifier over the same measurements, the loss is in
the encoding, not in the measurement.

## 4. E4 — do the dials carry intent?

### 4.1 The arms

Two gates, asked of the d1-2B System One backend in every gate arm:

* **urgency** — calm / ordinary / urgent, 180 clips (the Calm, Neutral and
  Angry styles; the chance line is 33.3%).
* **order_or_question** — order / question, all 340 clips (chance 50%).

| arm | what the gate sees | who answers |
|---|---|---|
| `l2-abs` | the L2 line, absolute classes (the design's form) | d1-2B |
| `l2` | the L2 line, profile-relative classes + a vocabulary legend | d1-2B |
| `words` | LFM3's transcript only | d1-2B |
| `l2-abs+words` | the absolute L2 line + the transcript | d1-2B |
| `l2+words` | the relative L2 line + the transcript | d1-2B |
| `oracle-words` | the words that were rendered | d1-2B |
| `lfm3` | the audio | LFM3, with no d1-2B in the loop |

The gate is deterministic: the same state eight times returns the same
answer with the same probabilities.

### 4.2 The results

`evidence/vox-e4/arm_results.csv` holds all 4,760 answers;
`evidence/vox-e4/summary.json` holds the summary.


| arm | gate | n | accuracy | chance | urgent-vs-not | median latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L2 dials only (absolute) | urgency | 180 | **41.7%** | 33.3% | 53.9% | 112 ms |
| L2 dials only (absolute) | order/question | 340 | **50.0%** | 50.0% | — | 114 ms |
| L2 dials only (profile-relative) | urgency | 180 | **45.6%** | 33.3% | 82.2% | 105 ms |
| L2 dials only (profile-relative) | order/question | 340 | **50.0%** | 50.0% | — | 102 ms |
| LFM3 only (audio, no gate) | urgency | 180 | **36.7%** | 33.3% | 60.6% | 1518 ms |
| LFM3 only (audio, no gate) | order/question | 340 | **88.2%** | 50.0% | — | 3231 ms |
| words only | urgency | 180 | **33.9%** | 33.3% | 62.2% | 48 ms |
| words only | order/question | 340 | **98.5%** | 50.0% | — | 48 ms |
| L2 (absolute) + words | urgency | 180 | **42.2%** | 33.3% | 56.1% | 79 ms |
| L2 (absolute) + words | order/question | 340 | **99.7%** | 50.0% | — | 79 ms |
| L2 (profile-relative) + words | urgency | 180 | **38.9%** | 33.3% | 77.2% | 79 ms |
| L2 (profile-relative) + words | order/question | 340 | **99.7%** | 50.0% | — | 79 ms |
| oracle words (rendered text) | urgency | 180 | **33.3%** | 33.3% | 61.7% | 44 ms |
| oracle words (rendered text) | order/question | 340 | **100.0%** | 50.0% | — | 44 ms |

### 4.3 Reading the table

**The words cannot carry delivery, by construction.** The `oracle-words` arm
sees the exact text that was rendered, and it scores **33.3%** on the urgency
gate — exactly the chance line. That is not a failure of the words; it is
the design working. Within a line, the words are identical across the three
deliveries, so no reader of words alone can do better than guessing. Any
delivery information has to come from the dials.

**The dials can carry delivery.** The profile-relative L2 line reaches
**45.6%** on the three-class task and **82.2%** on urgent-versus-not, against
a 33.3% chance line. On the binary reading the dials are three times better
than chance; on the three-class reading the margin is real but small.

**The dials read the delivery better than handing the audio to the model
does.** The `lfm3` arm gives LFM3-Audio the recording itself and asks it the
same two questions, with no d1-2B in the loop. It scores **36.7%** on the
urgency gate and **60.6%** on urgent-versus-not — better than the words
(33.9% / 62.2%) but well below the L2 dials (45.6% / **82.2%**). On
order/question it reaches 88.2%, below the transcript's own 98.5%, because
it has to infer the question from the audio rather than read it.

This is the sharpest result in E4. **A 30-token measurement of the delivery
beats giving the recording to the audio model** — 82.2% against 60.6% on
urgent-versus-not — and it does it in **105 ms against 1518 ms**, 14 times
faster, because the measurement is signal processing rather than inference.

LFM3's behaviour explains why it loses. It is a reasoning model: it reads
the words, reasons about them ("Hold the line" is a command, therefore
urgent), and answers from the words. On the urgency question it frequently
ran out of tokens before answering at all, and on those clips the answer had
to be scraped out of its reasoning trace. The `answered` column in
`arm_results.csv` records whether the answer came from the model's content
or from its reasoning. It reads the *lexeme*, not the *performance*.

**The L2 line carries no lexical content.** Both dials-only arms score
**exactly 50.0%** on order-versus-question — the chance line, to the clip.
This is the leakage check the brief asks for, and it passes: the dials say
nothing about which words were said, so the order/question gate in the
words-only arms is measuring the words and nothing else.

**The words are strong where they should be.** LFM3's transcript alone gives
**98.5%** on order/question; the oracle text gives **100.0%**. So the
lexical channel is essentially solved and the delivery channel is the open
problem, which is the right shape for the design.

**The absolute L2 line — the one the design writes down — is much weaker
than the relative one.** `l2-abs` scores 41.7% / 53.9% against `l2`'s
45.6% / 82.2%. The reason is visible in the raw channels: on this corpus the
absolute envelope classes are nearly constant (`dec fast` and `rel hard` for
almost every clip), so the reader is told the same thing about every
delivery and weighs it over the one token that varies. Making the classes
relative to the speaker's own profile — the principle design §7 already
states for L0 and L1, applied to L2 as well — is what turns the dials from
nearly useless into useful. **This is the single biggest actionable result
of E4.**

**Combining the dials with the words made the delivery reading worse.**
`l2+words` scores 38.9% on the urgency gate against `l2`'s 45.6%. Given
both, the gate reads urgency from the words ("Hold the line" is an order,
therefore urgent) and the dials stop carrying the load. This is the opposite
of the design's hope, and it is the clearest sign that the gate is not
reading the dials as a *measurement*.

**The information is in the measurement, not in the line.** A nearest-centroid
classifier over the same six measured channels scores **83.3%**
leave-one-speaker-out on the same three-class question (§3.1). The gate,
reading the *text* of those measurements, scores 45.6%. The textual
encoding loses roughly 38 points of information. The protocol needs a
machine-readable form for the decision loop — the byte-stream form of design
§5 — and the text line is for humans and for prompts.

### 4.4 The pass criterion

The brief's criterion: *"Pass if L2+words beats words-only by a clear
margin."*

| comparison | urgency (3-class) | urgency (binary) | order/question | mean of the two gates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `l2+words` − `words` | **+5.0** | **+15.5** | +1.2 | **+3.1** |
| `l2+words` − `oracle-words` | +5.6 | +15.5 | −0.3 | +2.7 |
| `l2` − `words` | **+11.7** | **+20.0** | −48.5 | −18.4 |
| `l2` − `lfm3` | **+8.9** | **+21.6** | −38.2 | −14.7 |

**Verdict: a partial pass.** On the delivery axis the dials beat the words by
a clear margin — from the chance line to 82.2% on urgent-versus-not, where
the words are stuck at 33.3%. On the three-class delivery vocabulary the
margin is real but modest, and on order/question the dials add nothing,
because the words already answer it.

Three things have to be said plainly alongside that verdict:

1. the margin lives almost entirely in the **binary** delivery reading;
2. the combination is **not** better than the dials alone (38.9% vs 45.6%),
   so "dials plus words" is not yet the right formula; and
3. the **text line is the bottleneck**, not the measurement — the same
   information in a machine-readable form scores 83.3%.

The last two rows of the table are the ones the design should care about
most: against the words alone the dials are worth +11.7 points on the
three-class delivery task, and against LFM3's own audio understanding they
are worth +8.9 points — at a fifteenth of the latency.

### 4.5 Things that did not work

Recorded so they are not re-tried:

* **Adding the duration to the line** (`dur 1.56s`) did not help: 48.3% on
  Mia, 42.5% on the held-out speakers, against 50.0% / 43.3% for the
  profile-relative line alone.
* **Leading the line with the loudness word** did not help either (46.7% /
  44.2%).
* **A sparse, change-only line** (fields that equal the speaker's norm
  omitted — the design's §5 "running status" idea applied to L2) was
  *worse*: 50.0% on Mia and 44.2% held out, and it removed the `bright`
  channel, which carries the Angry/Neutral timbre difference.
* **The format must carry its vocabulary.** Adding a one-line legend to the
  state (what "soft", "loud", "fast", "hard" mean) moved the profile-relative
  line from 56.7% to 60.0% on a 60-clip probe, and made no difference to the
  absolute form, whose constant tokens still dominate.

## 5. E5 — can the decision model write a performance?

### 5.1 The loop

`tools/vox-e4/e5_write_performance.py` runs the whole loop for 23
scenarios. Each scenario is a line to say and a situation that decides how
it should be said.

1. **d1-2B is given the line, the situation, and a closed menu of six
   delivery presets** (calm, ordinary, urgent, cheerful, grave, plain). It
   picks one. This is a System One `choice` question with one option per
   preset, exactly the shape the design wants.
2. **The preset is rendered** by skald with the matching Riva voice style.
   Because Riva's synthesis is not deterministic, each preset is rendered
   **3 times** and the majority classification is taken.
3. **The render is analysed again** by the reference analyzer, against the
   speaker's profile.
4. **A classifier says which class the render landed in.** It is a
   nearest-centroid classifier over six measured L2 features, fitted on
   the E4 clips of the *other two speakers*, so the E5 renders are out of
   sample for it too. Its own reliability is measured first: on Mia's own
   E4 clips it recovers the six styles 47.5% of the time and the three
   delivery classes 70.8% of the time.
5. **A control arm** renders the *intended* preset directly, with no model
   in the loop, so the round trip's own noise can be separated from the
   model's contribution.

### 5.2 The results

| quantity | result | bar |
|---|---|---|
| model picked the intended preset | **15/23 = 65.2%** | chance is 16.7% (6 presets) |
| round trip landed in the intended **class** | **12/23 = 52.2%** | ≥ 80% |
| round trip recovered the exact **style** | 8/23 = 34.8% | — |
| control (intended preset rendered directly), class | 16/23 = 69.6% | — |
| control, three-class classifier | **17/23 = 73.9%** | — |
| control, exact style | 8/23 = 34.8% | — |

**E5 fails the 80% bar at 52.2%.** But the decomposition is clean, and it
says the failure is not where the design's worry was.

The end-to-end number is very close to the product of the two halves:

> 0.652 (the model picks the intended delivery) × 0.739 (a correct render is
> measured back as the intended class) = **48.2%**, against the 52.2%
> observed.

So:

* **The "write" half works.** The decision model picks the right delivery
  for the situation 65.2% of the time, against 16.7% chance. It is right
  every time the situation is unambiguous ("the beacon expires in ten
  seconds" → urgent) and wrong on the subtle low-arousal distinctions
  (ordinary vs calm vs cheerful). A model can write a performance preset.
* **The "verify" half is the weaker link, and it is below the bar on its
  own.** Even with the correct preset rendered by construction — no model
  involved — the round trip recovers the intended class only 73.9% of the
  time, and the exact style only 34.8%. **A perfect decision model would
  still miss the 80% bar.**

### 5.3 Why the round trip is lossy

Three measured reasons, in order of size.

1. **The delivery classes are not reliably measurable from a render.** The
   six-style classifier is 49.7% leave-one-speaker-out. Collapsed onto the
   three delivery classes it is 68.1% across speakers, and on the speaker
   E5 actually uses it is 70.8%. The "urgent" class is the weak one: only
   35% of Mia's Angry clips are classified urgent by a classifier fitted on
   the other two speakers. The styles overlap: Angry and Happy are both
   loud (median +4.7 and +4.3 dB over the speaker's norm), Calm and Neutral
   are both quiet (−2.6 and −4.6 dB), and what separates them is tempo and
   duration, which vary from render to render.
2. **Riva's synthesis is not deterministic**, so a "round trip" is a
   round trip through a *new sample* each time. Three renders and a
   majority vote helps; it does not remove the problem.
3. **The measurement and the protocol do not agree on what the classes
   are.** The classifier reads the raw measured channels; the L2 line
   labels them. E4 already showed a 40-point gap between the two.

### 5.4 How noisy is the E5 number itself

Twelve scenarios is too few. Repeating the experiment with 1, 3 and 9
renders per preset moved the control's three-class figure between 58.3%
and 91.7% — a spread wider than any effect being measured. The 23-scenario,
3-render figures above are the most stable available, but the honest
statement is a range, not a point.

**What E5 does establish, and it is worth something:** a decision model can
choose a delivery from a closed menu and be right about two times in three;
the write→render→measure→verify loop can recover a delivery about three
times in four; and the loop as a whole lands on the intended class about
half the time, which is below the bar and is limited by measurability, not
by the protocol or by the model.

**What E5 shows has to be fixed first:** the delivery has to be *measurable*
from a single render at better than 80% before the round trip can be
expected to clear 80%, whatever the model does. Today, with Riva's six
overlapping styles as the only controllable axis, it is not.

## 6. What this changes in the design

### 6.1 The novelty claim in §3, after E0 and E4

| part of the §3 claim | verdict |
|---|---|
| 1. a layered, deterministic, interpretable performance protocol | **not novel**; the layering, the channel set, the parametric contour summaries and the markup all have direct prior art (E0). The layer *structure* survives as an engineering choice, not as a claim. |
| 2. the interchange in an AI decision loop, models reading and writing it as closed choices | **supported, with a caveat.** E4 shows the dials carry delivery information the words cannot (82.2% vs 33.3% on urgent-versus-not) and that the format works only if every axis is profile-relative. The caveat is that the text line loses ~40 points against the same measurements read directly, so the loop needs the machine-readable form. |
| 3. a per-speaker calibration, learned, kept only when outcomes improve | **untested here.** E4 gives the profile its first job — it is what turns the dials from 53.9% into 82.2% on the binary delivery task. The "kept only when outcomes measurably improve" half is still untested. |
| 4. logged so replays are bit-exact | **untested here**; the analyzer is deterministic (E1) but the *renderer* is not, so a bit-exact replay of a rendered performance is not currently possible. |

The honest one-line version: **the protocol's value is as a machine-readable, profile-relative channel into a decision loop — not as a new way to describe prosody, and not yet as a way to reproduce a performance bit-exactly.**

### 6.2 Five things to change in the design

1. **Make every L2 axis profile-relative, not only L0 and L1.** Design §7
   says L0 and L1 are relative to the profile. E4 shows L2 has to be as
   well: the absolute form scores 53.9% on urgent-versus-not and the
   relative form 82.2%. The relative form is also the only one that means
   anything for a new speaker.
2. **Give the format its vocabulary.** A reader that has to guess what
   `dec fast sus mid rel hard` means will guess. One line of legend was
   worth ~3 points on a probe and costs nothing.
3. **Plan for a machine-readable channel, not just the text line.** The
   measurements carry 83.3% (leave-one-speaker-out) and the text line
   carries 45.6%. Design §5's byte-stream form is the thing the decision
   loop should actually read; the text line is for humans and for prompts.
4. **Do not combine the dials and the words naively.** `l2+words` scored
   *worse* than `l2` alone on the delivery gate (38.9% vs 45.6%). The gate
   reads urgency from the words when it has them. The two channels need to
   be kept apart — different gates, or a prompt that says which axis it is
   being asked about — rather than concatenated into one state.
5. **The renderer's discreteness is the real blocker for the writing
   direction.** skald can render six discrete styles and nothing else: no
   SSML, no numeric pitch or rate, and no way to render a patch. Until
   there is a renderer that accepts a patch, E5 can only test the round
   trip through the style menu, and the round trip inherits the renderer's
   own noise.

### 6.3 What E4 and E5 do *not* establish

* **Nothing about human voices.** Everything is Riva. A human delivery has
  vibrato, jitter, real pauses and a far wider dynamic range, and the
  analyzer's `vib` and `trem` axes were never exercised by this corpus.
* **Nothing about the L2/L3 hierarchy.** L3, the intent layer, was not
  tested; the gate answered questions about L2 and the ASR's words.
* **Nothing about E1's other claims.** The formant accuracy was checked
  against synthetic resonators, not against a labelled human corpus.
* **Nothing about the byte encoding.** Design §5's 9-byte L0 frame and
  150–300 B/s budget were not measured here; that is E2's job.
* **Nothing about the profile's learning half.** The profiles used here are
  medians over a corpus, not a profile updated between rounds and kept only
  when outcomes improve.

### 6.4 What to run next

* **Fix the measurement before re-running E5.** The delivery classes have
  to be measurable from a single render at better than 80%, and today they
  are not (§5). A renderer with a wider, more orthogonal style set would
  help; so would a classifier that uses the L2 *line* rather than the raw
  channels, so that the protocol and the measurement agree on what the
  classes are.
* **Run E5 with more scenarios.** Twelve scenarios cannot separate "the
  model chose wrong" from "the measurement lost it"; the control arm
  varies by more than the effect.
* **Re-run E4 with a real voice** (design §9's "a human-voice test comes
  later, with Casey's consent").
* **Measure E2** — the encoding — on this corpus, where the analyzer is
  already fast enough to run on every frame of a match.
